    The data behind the conclusion

    So Meat peaked at 60.5 kg per person in 1987 and is about 16% lower today. Drinking milk fell 19% since 2017, while cheese rose 14% since 2007. And in just two years the flexitarian middle grew by 45%.

    Who is eating less meat, and why?

    The heart of the story: few Swiss quit meat, but many eat less of it. How many, who, and why?

    Now the numbers: how the Swiss are moving along this scale

    So Few Swiss give up meat completely, but about 1 in 4 households now eat flexitarian. Climate, animals and health push the change; taste, habit and cheese slow it down.

    How these plates were made

    Country plates come from FAO food supply data: calories per person per day for each food group (Our World in Data, 1961–2023). We turned calories into an estimated share of the plate by weight, using typical energy densities (for example vegetables about 0.3 kcal per gram, grains 1.6, meat 2.4, dairy 1.0). Oils, sugar and drinks are left out. Food supply is what is available in a country, not exactly what people eat: waste is included.

    Food pyramid plate: our translation of the Swiss food pyramid (BLV/SGE, 2024) into plate shares. Planet-friendly plate: the EAT-Lancet planetary health diet. Average Swiss man: the menuCH survey (2014–15).

    Week planner dishes are estimated shares of typical recipes, not measured data. Climate and land numbers are rough averages per food group from Poore and Nemecek (2018). Read all plates as good estimates, not exact measurements.

    Sources and notes

    Prototype for a student project. Meat figures are Proviande "supply per person", which includes bones and waste. Proviande estimates people actually eat about 100 g a day. The 2023–2025 meat values and the 1950s value are estimates worked out from published percentage changes. Plates and dish portions are illustrations, not measured data. In the plate game, targets are shares of daily portions (Swiss pyramid) or of grams (EAT-Lancet reference diet), and the "average Swiss man" plate is worked out from menuCH intake by weight, so the comparison is approximate. Country comparisons use different methods, so read them as rough. The St. Gallen study was co-funded by Danone.
